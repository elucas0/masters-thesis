\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{chap:results-discussion}

This chapter reports the experiments that evaluate the three research questions of Section~\ref{sec:research-objective}: whether a measurable sensor domain gap exists in the latent space of a geospatial foundation model (RQ1), whether cross-architecture distillation from that model can produce a sensor-invariant compact student (RQ2), and whether doing so is preferable to simply training the compact model on the target sensor directly (RQ3).
Following the ablations summarised in Section~\ref{sec:configuration}, all results below use a single configuration -- distillation with domain-specific batch normalization -- and the comparison of interest throughout is against a supervised baseline of identical architecture trained without any distillation.

\section{Experimental Configuration}
\label{sec:configuration}

The teacher is TerraMind~\cite{jakubik2025terramind} at the \texttt{base} scale, fine-tuned on the Sentinel-2 branch of the triplet dataset (Section~\ref{sec:triplets}) for the eleven-class WorldCover LULC task~\cite{zanaga2022worldcover}, and frozen thereafter.
The student is a U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015unet} with $9.85$\,M parameters, trained from random initialisation; no pre-trained weights are available for this backbone, which is a material constraint returned to in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.
Both models consume the seven-band Blue--NIR stack under the per-domain normalization protocol of Section~\ref{sec:normalization}.

Two elements of the configuration deserve to be stated explicitly, because both were arrived at empirically and both materially change the results.

\paragraph{Domain-specific batch normalization.}
The student carries a separate set of batch-normalization~\cite{ioffe2015batchnorm} statistics and affine parameters for each sensor, with all convolutional weights shared, following Chang et al.~\cite{chang2019dsbn}.
Beyond its role as a domain-adaptation mechanism, this corrects a train/test inconsistency inherent to the two-branch training scheme: the two sensors are forwarded in separate passes, so during training each pass is normalized by its own domain's batch statistics, while a single shared set of running averages -- a blend of both domains -- is what would be used at inference.
The encoder would therefore never encounter at test time the normalization under which it was optimised.

\paragraph{Class-imbalanced task loss.}
The WorldCover class prior on this dataset is severely imbalanced: the three rarest classes (snow/ice, mangroves, moss/lichen) together account for $1.2\%$ of labelled pixels but, under a macro-averaged mIoU, for $27\%$ of the metric.
Under unweighted cross-entropy the student never predicted these classes at all, scoring exactly zero IoU on them in training as well as validation -- a failure to learn rather than overfitting.
All runs reported here therefore use focal loss~\cite{lin2017focal} with $\gamma = 2$ and inverse-square-root class weighting derived from the measured pixel frequencies, a milder variant of the effective-number scheme of Cui et al.~\cite{cui2019classbalanced}.

Contrastive representation-alignment objectives (Section~\ref{sec:contrastive-mechanism}) were implemented and ablated but are disabled in all results below; their weights are zero throughout.
That ablation is reported separately and is not the subject of this chapter.

\section{Sensor Gap in TerraMind Latent Space (RQ1)}
\label{sec:rq1-results}

The first question is whether the domain gap is visible in the representation itself, rather than only in downstream accuracy.
Embeddings were extracted for $800$ co-registered test patches from both sensors and compared under three measures: paired cosine similarity between the two views of the same ground, proxy $\mathcal{A}$-distance (PAD)~\cite{bendavid2010theory}, and a cross-domain probe-transfer test in which a linear classifier fitted on Sentinel-2 embeddings is applied to $\Phi$-sat-2 embeddings of the same patches.

The teacher's representation does not transfer across the sensor pair.
Paired cosine similarity between the two views of the same ground is $0.098$ -- the two sensors are close to orthogonal in its embedding space -- and a linear probe fitted on its Sentinel-2 features retains only $33\%$ of its accuracy when applied to $\Phi$-sat-2 features ($0.754 \rightarrow 0.250$).
Figure~\ref{fig:embeddings-paired} shows this directly: grey lines join the two sensor views of each patch, and in the teacher's projection they stretch across the entire embedding (median link length $6.15$ UMAP~\cite{mcinnes2018umap} units).

Per-layer analysis locates the gap.
PAD between the two domains is $1.90$--$1.98$ at every transformer block, including the first, on a scale bounded at $2$.
A gap that were semantic in origin would be expected to emerge in the deeper, more abstract layers; one present from the first block instead points to low-level radiometry.
This is consistent with the spectral analysis of Section~\ref{sec:gap-measurement}, which found that the two sensors' digital numbers differ by more than an order of magnitude and that their per-band shapes differ in ways a single gain cannot reconcile.
It is worth noting that this ordering is the reverse of the resolution-driven account emphasised in related on-orbit work~\cite{du2025onorbitdemonstrationgeospatialfoundation}, since $\Phi$-sat-2's $4.75$\,m ground sampling distance is finer than Sentinel-2's.

\section{Sensor Invariance in the Distilled Student (RQ2)}
\label{sec:rq2-results}

The distilled student inverts every one of these measurements.
Paired cosine similarity rises from $0.098$ to $0.948$, and probe transfer from $33\%$ to $97\%$ ($0.663 \rightarrow 0.646$): a classifier fitted on the student's Sentinel-2 features is very nearly as accurate on its $\Phi$-sat-2 features.
In Figure~\ref{fig:embeddings-paired} the paired links collapse to $0.40$ UMAP units, a fifteen-fold reduction.
The downstream consequence is visible in the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:dsbn-vs-baseline}: the student's accuracy on the two sensors differs by only $0.032$ mIoU on the test split, against a teacher whose representation does not usefully transfer at all.

One measurement in this set behaves counter-intuitively and is worth reporting rather than suppressing, because it illustrates a trap in evaluating this class of method.
Raw PAD is maximal for the student ($2.000$, versus $1.955$ for the teacher): a linear probe separates the two domains perfectly.
This is an artefact of the mechanism itself.
Domain-specific normalization gives each sensor its own affine transform, so a constant per-channel offset distinguishes the domains by construction, and a linear probe finds it trivially regardless of how well the semantic content is aligned.
Standardising each domain on its own statistics -- which removes exactly the first and second moments that a per-domain affine can express -- drops the probe to chance for both models.
Figure~\ref{fig:pad-decision} shows the underlying quantity: in the raw setting the two domains' probe scores are separated by an empty margin, and after per-domain standardisation they collapse onto one another.

PAD is therefore not a valid instrument for a model with domain-specific normalization, even though it is scale-free and hence otherwise well suited to comparing representations of different dimensionality.
Scale-free is not affine-free.
Probe transfer, which asks the functional question directly, is the appropriate measure and is the one reported above.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_pad_decision_scores.png}
    \caption{Distribution of the domain probe's out-of-fold decision score, the quantity from which PAD is computed. Separated modes imply a probe that never errs (PAD $\rightarrow 2$); overlapping modes imply chance (PAD $\rightarrow 0$). After standardising each domain on its own statistics (right column) the separability vanishes for both models, showing that all of it was per-channel mean and scale rather than representational structure.}
    \label{fig:pad-decision}
\end{figure}

This result should be stated precisely.
With domain-specific normalization the encoder is domain-adaptive rather than domain-invariant: convolutional weights are shared, normalization statistics are not, and the target-sensor branch must be selected at inference.
The defensible claim is that after per-domain normalization, structure learned on the source sensor is $97\%$ readable on the target -- which is weaker than a single sensor-agnostic encoder, but is the property an on-board deployment actually requires, since the acquiring instrument is always known.

\section{Distillation Against a Supervised Baseline (RQ3)}
\label{sec:rq3-results}

Sensor invariance is only useful if it buys accuracy that a simpler method would not.
The control is a student of identical architecture trained directly on labelled $\Phi$-sat-2 with no teacher, no second sensor and no distillation, holding constant the split, normalization, augmentation, task loss, optimiser, schedule and early-stopping criterion.

At a budget of $10{,}000$ labelled patches, distillation wins clearly (Table~\ref{tab:main-results}, Figure~\ref{fig:dsbn-vs-baseline}).
Best validation mIoU is $0.4532$ against $0.3989$, and on matched epochs the distilled student is ahead by $0.0227$ mIoU on average, winning $54$ of $71$ epochs (paired $t$-test, $p = 1.1\times10^{-8}$).
The advantage is not present early -- the baseline leads for roughly the first twenty epochs -- and then widens monotonically, which is why an earlier run truncated at $50$ epochs substantially understated it.

Per-class results (Figure~\ref{fig:per-class}) show the gain is broad rather than concentrated: distillation is ahead on nine of eleven classes, most markedly on mangroves ($+0.19$), herbaceous wetland ($+0.12$), grassland ($+0.12$) and cropland ($+0.10$).
Frequency-weighted IoU moves in the same direction as the macro average ($0.5132$ against $0.4467$), so the result is not an artefact of rare-class weighting.

Two costs qualify this.
Distillation requires $7.9$ minutes per epoch against the baseline's $3.3$, a factor of $2.4$, dominated by the frozen teacher's forward pass and the second student branch.
At a fixed compute budget rather than a fixed epoch count the comparison is much closer, and the baseline may be preferable.
Separately, the teacher itself reaches $0.5788$ mIoU on Sentinel-2, so the distilled student closes only part of a substantial capacity gap; compression, not merely domain adaptation, remains the binding constraint.

\section{The Advantage Reverses at Small Label Budgets}
\label{sec:label-crossover}

Distillation and unsupervised domain adaptation are usually motivated by label scarcity in the target domain~\cite{kouw2019introductiondomainadaptationtransfer}, so the expectation is that the advantage above should grow as target labels are removed.
It does the opposite.

At $1{,}000$ labelled patches the supervised baseline is ahead by every measure: best validation mIoU $0.2916$ against $0.2679$, test mIoU $0.2707$ against $0.2635$, and on matched epochs the distilled student wins only $8$ of $58$ epochs (paired $t$-test, $p = 1.5\times10^{-14}$).
It also does so at roughly a third of the cost, and with the larger budget available to it -- the distilled run used its full hundred epochs without triggering early stopping, while the baseline stopped at sixty-seven.
Figure~\ref{fig:label-crossover} shows the crossover; both budgets are evaluated on the identical fixed $1{,}000$-patch validation split, so the two points are directly comparable.

Both directions are strongly significant, which makes this a genuine crossover rather than noise at one end.
A plausible reading is that the distilled configuration carries substantially more machinery -- a second forward pass, doubled normalization parameters, and a distillation term pulling toward a teacher that is itself imperfect at $0.5788$ mIoU -- and that below some label threshold the cost of that machinery exceeds the value of the teacher's signal.
This is offered as a hypothesis: with two budgets the crossing point is bracketed but not located, and intermediate budgets would be required to characterise it.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What domain-specific normalization actually contributed}

The improvement from domain-specific normalization is larger than a pure domain-alignment effect would predict, and its shape is informative: accuracy on the source sensor improved by a similar margin to the target ($+0.116$ against $+0.185$ test mIoU relative to the preceding configuration).
A mechanism that only aligned domains should have improved the target branch alone.
The most likely explanation is the train/test normalization inconsistency described in Section~\ref{sec:configuration}: prior configurations were evaluated through a normalization path the encoder had never been optimised under, which depressed both branches.
On that reading, part of what is reported here as a domain-adaptation gain is the repair of a latent defect in the two-branch training scheme -- a caveat that does not diminish the practical result but does bear on how much of it should be attributed to domain adaptation per se.

\subsection{Two measurement pitfalls}
\label{sec:measurement-pitfalls}

Two artefacts encountered during this work are worth recording, as both would silently corrupt a comparison of exactly this kind.

First, the macro-averaged mIoU implemented by standard libraries excludes classes with zero union from its average, so its denominator depends on which classes a given model happens to predict.
Where a rare class is absent from a small evaluation split, two models are then averaged over different numbers of classes and their mIoU are not comparable -- an effect that inflated an apparent advantage by roughly a factor of three in an early version of the label-budget experiment.
All results in this chapter are reported on a fixed denominator over all eleven classes.

Second, and relatedly, an evaluation split sized as a fixed fraction of the training split makes a label-scarcity study vary its measurement and its treatment simultaneously.
At the smallest budget this left one hundred evaluation patches, in which the rarest class does not occur at all.
Evaluation splits are held fixed and independent of the training budget throughout.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

All experiments use at most $10{,}000$ of the $202{,}582$ available training patches -- under $5\%$ -- so the crossover of Section~\ref{sec:label-crossover} is characterised entirely within the low-data regime, and the ranking at full scale is untested.
Single seeds are used throughout; epoch-to-epoch validation variance is of the order of $0.02$ mIoU, so differences below roughly $0.03$ are resolved here only by paired testing across epochs, not by independent replication.
The student is trained from random initialisation because no pre-trained weights exist for this backbone, which plausibly accounts for a substantial share of the remaining $0.15$ mIoU gap to the teacher and confounds any claim that the gap is attributable to domain shift.
Finally, the work is scoped to a single sensor pair, so the finding that the gap is radiometric rather than resolution-driven (Section~\ref{sec:rq1-results}) should not be generalised beyond it.

\subsection{Interpretation of the flood-robustness result}
\label{sec:discussion-comparison}

The mild flood-IoU degradation observed across the simulated degradation ladder (Section~\ref{sec:degradation-pipeline}) is not unique to this thesis.
Du et al.~\cite{du2025onorbitdemonstrationgeospatialfoundation} independently report that flood detection was anomalously robust to the sensor domain gap on Kanyini, in contrast to an approximately $47\%$ drop in cloud-detection performance on the same platform.
Two independent studies observing the same pattern is worth foregrounding: water's strong near-infrared absorption contrast plausibly survives sensor noise and band mismatch better than the texture- and boundary-dependent cues that cloud or general land-cover classification rely on.
This has a direct consequence for how this thesis should be read: flood segmentation may understade the severity of the domain-gap problem, which is part of why the LULC task was adopted as the primary benchmark.

\subsection{Positioning with \texorpdfstring{$\Phi$}{Phi}satNet}

$\Phi$satNet (Section~\ref{sec:phisatnet-background}) reports that a purpose-built, non-transformer model trained from scratch on a mission-specific corpus matches or exceeds unconstrained transformer-based GeoFMs on several on-board tasks.
This raises a reasonable objection: if a bespoke lightweight model already performs well without any of the machinery developed here, what is gained by adapting TerraMind instead?

The results of Section~\ref{sec:label-crossover} sharpen rather than settle that question.
The answer offered by this thesis is a label-efficiency bet -- that large-scale multi-modal pre-training generalises to new $\Phi$-sat-2 tasks with fewer labels than a narrower curated pre-training suite requires -- and the crossover measured here shows that such a bet is budget-dependent in a way that must be quantified rather than assumed.
A matched few-shot comparison against $\Phi$satNet on a shared task and label budget remains the appropriate test and has not been run.

\subsection{Practical recommendations}

Three recommendations follow.
First, missions intended to host on-board foundation models should prioritise collecting co-registered calibration data across the sensor pair the mission needs to bridge, early in operational life; this is what made the measurements in this chapter possible at all.
Second, where the deployed sensor is known at inference -- which on board a satellite it always is -- domain-specific normalization is a very high value-for-effort intervention: it is a few hundred additional parameters and required no change to the training objective.
Third, the label budget should be measured before adopting a distillation pipeline, since below the crossover of Section~\ref{sec:label-crossover} the simpler supervised baseline was both more accurate and roughly three times cheaper.
