\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{chap:results-discussion}

This chapter reports the experiments that evaluate the three research questions of Section~\ref{sec:research-objective}.
All the results below use the single configuration set out in Section~\ref{sec:configuration}, which is a distillation with domain-specific batch normalization, and the comparison of interest throughout is against a supervised baseline of identical architecture trained without any distillation.

\section{Experimental Configuration}
\label{sec:configuration}

The teacher is TerraMind~\cite{jakubik2025terramind} at the \texttt{base} scale, fine-tuned on the Sentinel-2 branch of the triplet dataset (Section~\ref{sec:triplets}) for the eleven-class WorldCover \acref{LULC} task~\cite{zanaga2022worldcover}, and frozen thereafter.
The student is a U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015unet} with $9.85$\,M parameters, trained from a random initialisation, since no pre-trained weights are available for this backbone.
We return to this constraint in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.
Both models consume the seven-band Blue-\acref{NIR} stack under the per-domain normalization protocol of Section~\ref{sec:normalization}.

Two elements of this configuration need to be stated explicitly, since we arrived at both of them empirically and both of them change the results.

\paragraph{Domain-specific batch normalization.}
The student carries a separate set of batch-normalization~\cite{ioffe2015batchnorm} statistics and affine parameters for each sensor, with all convolutional weights shared, following Chang et al.~\cite{chang2019dsbn}.
Beyond its role as a domain-adaptation mechanism, this also corrects a train/test inconsistency that is inherent to the two-branch training scheme.
The two sensors are forwarded in separate passes, so during the training each pass is normalized by the batch statistics of its own domain, while a single shared set of running averages, which is a blend of the two domains, is what would be used at inference.
The encoder would therefore never encounter at test time the normalization under which it was optimised.

\paragraph{Class-imbalanced task loss.}
The WorldCover class prior on this dataset is severely imbalanced: the three rarest classes (snow/ice, mangroves, moss/lichen) together account for $1.2\%$ of labelled pixels but, under a macro-averaged mIoU, for $27\%$ of the metric.
Under unweighted cross-entropy the student never predicted these classes at all, scoring exactly zero IoU on them in training as well as validation.
All the runs reported here therefore use a focal loss~\cite{lin2017focal} with $\gamma = 2$, and an inverse-square-root class weighting derived from the measured pixel frequencies, which is a milder variant of the effective-number scheme of Cui et al.~\cite{cui2019classbalanced}.

The contrastive representation-alignment objectives (Section~\ref{sec:contrastive-mechanism}) were implemented and ablated, but they are disabled in all the results below, since their weights are zero throughout.
That ablation is reported separately.

Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters} lists the settings of both methods.
Everything the two conditions could share is held constant: architecture, initialisation, split, seed, normalization, augmentation, task loss, optimiser, schedule, and early-stopping criterion are identical, and the methods differ only in the presence of the teacher, the second sensor branch, and domain-specific normalization.

\section{Sensor Gap in TerraMind Latent Space}
\label{sec:rq1-results}

The first question is whether the domain gap is visible in the representation itself, and not only in the downstream accuracy.
We extracted embeddings for $800$ co-registered test patches from both sensors, and compared them under three measures: the paired cosine similarity between the two views of the same ground, the proxy $\mathcal{A}$-distance (\acref{PAD})~\cite{bendavid2010theory}, and a cross-domain probe-transfer test in which a linear classifier fitted on Sentinel-2 embeddings is applied to the $\Phi$-sat-2 embeddings of the same patches.

The teacher's representation does not transfer across the sensor pair.
The paired cosine similarity between the two views of the same ground is $0.098$, so the two sensors are close to orthogonal in its embedding space, and a linear probe fitted on its Sentinel-2 features retains only $33\%$ of its accuracy once it is applied to the $\Phi$-sat-2 features ($0.754 \rightarrow 0.250$).
Figure~\ref{fig:embeddings-paired} shows this directly: grey lines join the two sensor views of each patch, and in the teacher's projection they stretch across the entire embedding (median link length $6.15$ \acref{UMAP}~\cite{mcinnes2018umap} units).

\acref{PAD} between the two domains is $1.90$--$1.98$ at every transformer block on a scale of $2$.
A gap that was semantic in origin would be expected to emerge in the deeper and more abstract layers, whereas one that is present from the first block points instead to low-level radiometry.
This is consistent with the spectral analysis of Section~\ref{sec:gap-measurement}, which found that the two sensors' digital numbers differ largely and that their per-band shapes differ heavily.
This analysis is also the reverse of the resolution-driven account emphasised in related on-orbit work~\cite{du2025onorbitdemonstrationgeospatialfoundation}, since the $4.75$\,m ground sampling distance of $\Phi$-sat-2 is finer than Sentinel-2's.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_embeddings_paired.png}
    \caption{\acs{UMAP} projection of pooled embeddings for $800$ co-registered test patches, teacher (left) and distilled student (right), each projected independently.
        Grey lines join the two sensor views of the same patch.
        In the teacher's space the paired views are far apart (median link $6.15$ units); in the student's they are effectively coincident ($0.40$ units).
    }
    \label{fig:embeddings-paired}
\end{figure}

A raw per-layer cosine-similarity check on the smaller TerraMind Tiny encoder, fine-tuned separately on real $\Phi$-sat-2 data as part of the earlier protocol described in Appendix~\ref{app:real-baseline}, is consistent with this picture (Figure~\ref{fig:cosine-sim-tiny}).
Similarity between paired Sentinel-2B and real-$\Phi$-sat-2 embeddings is near zero at the first block and rises through the middle of the network, mirroring the low-level-radiometry reading of the per-layer \acref{PAD} result above.
This is a supplementary observation, on a different model scale and with a different measure than the analysis above (a raw cosine similarity in place of \acref{PAD}), and we offer it as a consistency check rather than as a replication.

\section{Degradation effects on Downstream Task Gap}
\label{sec:ladder-results}

This section takes up the last part of the previous research question, which asks which of the physical differences between the two instruments is the one that produces the gap.
It builds on the measurements above, which established that a gap exists and that it is low-level in origin, and narrows the question to three candidates: radiometric noise, optical blur, and band misalignment.
The two instruments differ in their spectral response, their radiometric noise, their band registration and their ground sampling distance all at once, and no measurement on real pairs can separate those.
The simulated ladder of Section~\ref{sec:sen1floods-sim} makes the separation possible by degrading one factor at a time over identical scene content.
Three factors are varied over four severities each: sensor noise (\acref{SNR}), optical blur (\acref{PSF}), and band-to-band misalignment.
Table~\ref{tab:ladder-definition} gives the parameter values.
A severity axis common to all three is defined by setting level~4 to the operating point of the joint simulator configuration and halving the perturbation amount at each step down, so that a given level means the same fraction of full simulated degradation whichever factor is being varied.

We want to measure the domain gap by isolating the effect of each degradation factor.
Each model is trained once on the clean data, and the resulting checkpoint is then evaluated on the test split of every variant, so the degradation only occurs at inference.
Three encoder configurations are compared: the distilled student with its encoder frozen and its decoder trained ($5.14$\,M of $9.86$\,M parameters trainable), TerraMind \texttt{base} under the same frozen-encoder protocol ($7.92$\,M of $94.25$\,M), and TerraMind \texttt{base} fine-tuned end to end.
Each configuration is run for three seeds using the same model checkpoint across all test splits.

On clean data the distilled student is the strongest of the three, at $0.814$ IoU against $0.773$ for fine-tuned TerraMind and $0.739$ for frozen TerraMind.
That the smaller student outperforms the foundation model on this clean task can be attributed to its stronger local representation, whereas TerraMind is a general-purpose encoder given only a task head and a fine-tuning pass.
The comparison of interest here is the response to degradation.

Band misalignment is the only factor that degrades every configuration significantly, monotonically, and by a similar amount.
Its full-severity cost varies only between $-0.109$ and $-0.140$ IoU across models that differ in trainable parameters and by architecture family, and it is the only factor resolved at the mildest severity in all three columns, where a $1.25$-pixel shift already costs a small but reproducible amount.
Its seed-to-seed deviation is also the smallest in the table.
A perturbation whose cost barely changes with the model absorbing it is a damage to the input signal, rather than a weakness of any particular representation.
Band-to-band shifts corrupt the cross-band relationships on which a water/land decision rests, and no amount of encoder capacity recovers information that the shift has already destroyed.

Sensor noise produces the largest single degradation, but its magnitude is a property of the model rather than of the perturbation, and it is the least reproducible quantity in the table.
At full severity it costs the distilled student $-0.458$ IoU on average and fine-tuned TerraMind $-0.152$, a threefold difference that holds at every severity.

Optical blur is the least damaging factor in every configuration, but the three differ in how cleanly the effect is resolved.
For fine-tuned TerraMind it is indistinguishable from zero: four severities spanning a twenty-four-fold reduction in image gradient energy move the score by at most $0.008$ IoU.
For frozen TerraMind the response is monotone and uniformly negative but never clears the significance threshold, reaching $-0.025$ at full severity.
For the distilled student it is small but unambiguous, significant at all four severities with the tightest deviations in the table, rising from $-0.002$ to $-0.110$.
Blur is therefore real for the student and negligible for TerraMind, yet even for the student it remains the smallest of the three factors, costing a quarter of what noise costs at the same nominal severity.

This ordering of factors resolves the attribution ambiguity left open in Section~\ref{sec:rq1-results}.
The factor that models a resolution difference, which is the optical blur, is the one that costs almost nothing, while the radiometric factor and the band-registration factor are the ones carrying the effect.
The per-layer \acref{PAD} result located the gap in low-level radiometry rather than in semantics, and the ladder independently identifies the two low-level physical effects responsible, using a different task, a different measurement, and imagery in which scene content is held fixed by construction.
It also reinforces the point that the gap here is not resolution-driven, which is again the reverse of the emphasis found in related on-orbit work~\cite{du2025onorbitdemonstrationgeospatialfoundation}.

Repeating the sweep with each model trained on the same degraded condition it is evaluated on, instead of on clean data, recovers most of the loss (Table~\ref{tab:ladder-adaptation}).
$84\%$ of the noise penalty and $66\%$ of the misalignment penalty disappear, and the blur stops being a penalty, since it scores marginally above the clean baseline, like a mild augmentation.
The gap measured in Table~\ref{tab:ladder-domain-gap} is therefore largely an adaptation gap rather than an information-loss gap, at least for noise and blur.
Where the noise characteristics of the operating sensor are known in advance, simulating them during the training recovers most of the achievable performance, without any domain-adaptation machinery.
Misalignment is again the exception: a third of its cost survives training on the degraded condition, which is consistent with reading it as a factor that degrades the cross-band information.

Three limitations bound these results.
We vary the factors one at a time, whereas a real sensor pair presents all of them at once, and possibly interacting, so the ladder identifies which effects matter in isolation, and not how they compose.
The degradations are simulated, so the conclusion concerns the simulator's model of the instrument difference, and the absence of real labelled $\Phi$-sat-2 flood data (Section~\ref{sec:sen1floods-sim}) that makes it necessary.
Finally, the supervised U-Net baseline is not included in Table~\ref{tab:ladder-domain-gap}, so these results characterise the distilled and foundation-model encoders but do not establish whether distillation itself changed robustness relative to training on the target sensor directly.

\section{Sensor Adaptivity in the Distilled Student}
\label{sec:rq2-results}

The distilled student demonstrates the opposite behavior across these measurements.
The paired cosine similarity rises from $0.098$ to $0.948$, and the probe transfer from $33\%$ to $97\%$ ($0.663 \rightarrow 0.646$), which means that a classifier fitted on the student's Sentinel-2 features is very nearly as accurate on its $\Phi$-sat-2 features.
In Figure~\ref{fig:embeddings-paired} the paired links collapse to $0.40$ \acref{UMAP} units.
The downstream consequence is visible in the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:dsbn-vs-baseline}.
The accuracy of the student on the two sensors converges on parity: on the test split it scores $0.4481$ on $\Phi$-sat-2 against $0.4640$ on Sentinel-2, which is a residual difference of $-0.016$ mIoU.
Table~\ref{tab:invariance} collects the three measures side by side.

The teacher remains a stronger encoder within the source Sentinel-2 domain ($0.754$ against $0.663$ on same-domain probe accuracy). 
This performance drop shows the capacity bottleneck when distilling a large GeoFM into a lightweight U-Net. 
The student lacks the parameter space to fully retain the teacher's representations, sacrificing in-domain accuracy to achieve cross-sensor adapatation.

One measurement in this set behaves counter-intuitively, and it illustrates a real trap in evaluating this class of method.
The raw \acref{PAD} is maximal for the student ($2.000$, against $1.955$ for the teacher), which means that a linear probe separates the two domains perfectly.
This is an artefact of the mechanism itself, since the domain-specific normalization gives each sensor its own affine transform.
A constant per-channel offset distinguishes the domains by construction, and a linear probe finds it trivially regardless of how well the semantic content is aligned.
Standardising each domain on its own statistics removes the first and second moments that a per-domain affine can express, and it drops the probe to chance for both models.
Figure~\ref{fig:pad-decision} shows the underlying quantity: in the raw setting the two domains' probe scores are separated by an empty margin, and after per-domain standardisation they collapse onto one another.

\acref{PAD} is therefore not a valid measurement for a model that carries a domain-specific normalization, even though it is scale-free and otherwise well suited to comparing representations of different dimensionality.
The probe transfer, which asks the functional question directly, is the appropriate measure here, and it is the one we report above.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_pad_decision_scores.png}
    \caption{Distribution of the domain probe's out-of-fold decision score, the quantity from which \acs{PAD} is computed.
        Separated modes imply a probe that perfectly separates the domains (\acs{PAD} $\rightarrow 2$); overlapping modes imply chance (\acs{PAD} $\rightarrow 0$).
        After standardising each domain on its own statistics (right column) the separability disappears for both models, showing that all of it was per-channel mean and scale.
    }
    \label{fig:pad-decision}
\end{figure}

With a domain-specific normalization, the encoder is domain-adaptive rather than domain-invariant: the convolutional weights are shared but the normalization statistics are not, and the target-sensor branch has to be selected at inference.
It follows that after per-domain normalization, the structure learned on the source sensor is $97\%$ readable on the target one.
That is weaker than a single sensor-agnostic encoder, but it is the property that an on-board deployment requires, since the acquiring instrument is always known.

Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative} shows what that parity looks like on individual patches.
In the first three rows the two student columns are close to interchangeable: the same field boundaries, the same built-up extent, the same forest fragments, produced from two sensors whose raw digital numbers do not even share a scale (Section~\ref{sec:triplets}).
The student also recovers finer structure than the teacher does on the first row, where the teacher's map smooths the ridged vegetation pattern that both student branches keep.
The fourth row shows a failure case with a noisy real acquisition, making the $\Phi$-sat-2 branch collapse to permanent water and herbaceous wetland across most of the patch, while the Sentinel-2 branch of the same weights stays close to the label map.

\section{Distillation Against a Supervised Baseline}
\label{sec:rq3-results}

Sensor adaptivity is only useful if it improves accuracy over a simpler method.
The control for this is a student of identical architecture, trained directly on labelled $\Phi$-sat-2 with no teacher, no second sensor and no distillation.
Some parameters are held constant for fairness: the split, the normalization, the augmentation, the task loss, the optimiser, the schedule and the early-stopping criterion (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}).

At a budget of $10{,}000$ labelled patches the distilled student has $0.4607$ best validation mIoU against the baseline's $0.3720$, an improvement of $+0.0887$ and $0.4481$ against $0.3739$ on the test split (Table~\ref{tab:main-results}).
Figure~\ref{fig:dsbn-vs-baseline} shows the shape of that margin: the baseline is ahead during the first twenty-five epochs, then the two curves cross but the distilled run keeps climbing after the baseline has flattened and stalled due to early stopping.

The distilled run's best epoch is the $89$th of the $101$ it ran, and it exhausted the full budget without triggering the early stopping, so the result should be read as a lower bound on what this configuration reaches.
The baseline, by contrast, early-stopped at epoch $73$.

The per-class results (Figure~\ref{fig:per-class}) show that the gain is broad.
Distillation is ahead on ten of the eleven classes, most markedly on moss/lichen ($+0.148$), snow/ice ($+0.124$), mangroves ($+0.120$), cropland ($+0.112$), tree cover ($+0.097$) and grassland ($+0.096$).
Permanent water is the only exception ($-0.013$).
The frequency-weighted IoU moves in the same direction as the macro average ($0.523$ against $0.454$), so this result is not an artefact of the rare-class weighting introduced in Section~\ref{sec:configuration}.

This difference is a compression gap rather than a domain gap, since Section~\ref{sec:rq2-results} shows that the student performs similarly on the two domains, so what separates it from the teacher is its capacity and its initialisation.

\paragraph{Complexity against accuracy.}

The comparison above holds the architecture fixed and varies only the training signal, but for a space-constrained deployment the question is how much accuracy can be gained for a given parameter budget.
$\Phi$satNet has a clear advantage at only $351$\,k parameters compared to the U-Net that both methods use, and it is the class of model a mission would actually carry on board.
Table~\ref{tab:complexity} compares $\Phi$satNet trained on the same $10{,}000$ labelled patches and configuration as the supervised baseline, from two initialisations: the released weights for land-cover pre-training, and from scratch.

$\Phi$satNet drops to $0.042$ test mIoU compared to the supervised baseline and $0.117$ against the distilled student, but it has $28.1$ times fewer parameters.
The distilled student's own margin is bought for a small parameter cost: domain-specific normalization adds $6{,}848$ parameters to the shared architecture.
Section~\ref{sec:dsbn-contribution} treats how much this mechanism contributes to the accuracy gain.

Running the distillation of Section~\ref{sec:rq3-results} with the same settings (TerraMind teacher) but with $\Phi$satNet in place of the U-Net student gives $0.3268$ test mIoU, which is $0.005$ below the same architecture trained supervised and $0.005$ above it from a random initialisation.
All three $\Phi$satNet configurations sit inside a band of $0.01$, which is statistically insignificant.

A $0.35$\,M student has no representational room left for the teacher's soft targets to occupy once the task loss has claimed it, so the additional supervision has nothing to buy.
The per-class pattern supports this, because the U-Net's distillation gain is concentrated in the rare tail and that is precisely where the small student cannot follow: on snow and ice the distilled U-Net reaches $0.523$ against $\Phi$satNet's $0.248$, on mangroves $0.341$ against $0.094$, and on moss and lichen $0.263$ against $0.001$.
On the common classes the two are close, and on permanent water the small model is marginally ahead ($0.634$ against $0.631$).
This is the compression gap of Section~\ref{sec:rq3-results} measured rather than asserted: the residual between teacher and student does not shrink when the teacher's signal is handed to a smaller student, because what separates them is capacity and not supervision.

Fine-tuning from the released land-cover checkpoint, pre-trained on simulated $\Phi$-sat-2 imagery, beats the same architecture from a random initialisation by $0.0102$ test mIoU.
The two $\Phi$satNet rows differ only in their initialisation, and they show that the mission-specific pre-training does not buy a measurable advantage on this task.
Two further observations point the same direction.
Zero-shot with real $\Phi$-sat-2 on the released land-cover model scores between $0.0008$ and $0.0413$ mIoU across the ten published checkpoints, against the $0.0376$ that a uniform-random predictor scores on the eleven class frequencies.
On this task and at this budget it is therefore the architecture, and not the pre-training that accounts for the $\Phi$satNet result.

\section{Accuracy Drop with Few Labels}
\label{sec:label-crossover}

\acref{KD} and \acref{UDA} are usually motivated by a label scarcity in the target domain~\cite{kouw2019introductiondomainadaptationtransfer}, so we would expect the advantage above to grow as the target labels are removed; the opposite is what we observe.
At $1{,}000$ labelled patches the supervised baseline is ahead by every measure: best validation mIoU $0.2916$ against $0.2679$, test mIoU $0.2707$ against $0.2635$, and on matched epochs the distilled student leads in only $8$ of the $58$ epochs both runs share.
It also does so at roughly a third of the cost, and this even though the distilled run had the larger budget available to it: it used its full hundred epochs without triggering the early stopping, while the baseline stopped at sixty-seven.
Figure~\ref{fig:label-crossover} shows the crossover with both budgets evaluated on the identical fixed $1{,}000$-patch validation split.
One reading is that the distilled configuration carries substantial overhead, which is a second forward pass, doubled normalization parameters, and a distillation term pulling toward a teacher, and that below a certain label threshold the cost exceeds the value of the teacher's signal.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Domain-specific normalization contribution}
\label{sec:dsbn-contribution}

The improvement brought by the domain-specific normalization is larger than what a pure domain-alignment effect would predict.
The accuracy on the source sensor improved by a margin similar to the one on the target ($+0.116$ against $+0.185$ test mIoU relative to the preceding configuration).
A mechanism that only aligned domains should have improved the target branch alone.
The most likely explanation is that the train/test normalization inconsistency described in Section~\ref{sec:configuration}: the prior configurations were evaluated through a normalization path that the encoder had never been optimised under, which depressed the two branches at once.
On that reading, part of what is reported here as a domain-adaptation gain is the repair of a latent defect in the two-branch training scheme.
That caveat does not diminish the practical result, but it does bear on how much of it should be attributed to \acref{DA}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

All experiments use at most $10{,}000$ of the $202{,}582$ available training patches, under $5\%$, so the crossover of Section~\ref{sec:label-crossover} is characterised entirely within the low-data regime, and the ranking at full scale is untested.
Single seeds are used everywhere except the degradation-ladder evaluation of Section~\ref{sec:ladder-results}, so apart from that section no result in this chapter is supported by independent replication; epoch-to-epoch validation variance is of the order of $0.02$ mIoU, and differences below roughly $0.03$, which includes the small-budget end of the crossover in Section~\ref{sec:label-crossover}, are consequently not resolved.
Two further controls are outstanding at the time of writing: a shared-normalization distillation run isolating the contribution of domain-specific normalization (Section~\ref{sec:configuration}), and a recomputation of the invariance measures under shared and per-domain-standardised normalization (Section~\ref{sec:rq2-results}).
The student is trained from a random initialisation because no pre-trained weights exist for this backbone, and this plausibly accounts for a substantial share of the remaining $0.15$ mIoU gap to the teacher, and it confounds any claim that this gap is attributable to a domain shift.
Finally, the work is scoped to a single sensor pair, so the finding that the gap is radiometric rather than resolution-driven (Section~\ref{sec:rq1-results}) should not be generalised beyond it.

\subsection{Interpretation of the flood-robustness result}
\label{sec:discussion-comparison}

The flood-IoU degradation observed for the teacher across the simulated degradation ladder (Section~\ref{sec:ladder-results}, at worst $-0.152$ IoU under full-severity noise and $-0.008$ under full-severity blur) is expected.
Du et al.~\cite{du2025onorbitdemonstrationgeospatialfoundation} independently report that flood detection was anomalously robust to the sensor domain gap on Kanyini, in contrast to an approximately $47\%$ drop in cloud-detection performance on the same platform.
We observe the same pattern, and a plausible explanation is that the strong near-infrared absorption contrast of water survives the sensor noise and the band mismatch much better than the texture-dependent and boundary-dependent cues that cloud detection or general land-cover classification rely on.
Flood segmentation may then understate the severity of the domain-gap problem.

\subsection{Positioning with \texorpdfstring{$\Phi$}{Phi}satNet}
\label{sec:positioning-phisatnet}

$\Phi$satNet (Section~\ref{sec:phisatnet-background}) reports that a purpose-built, non-transformer model trained from scratch on a mission-specific corpus matches or exceeds unconstrained transformer-based GeoFMs on several on-board tasks~\cite{phisatnet}.
This raises a central operational question: if a lightweight model already achieves adequate performance, what is gained by adapting TerraMind ?

Table~\ref{tab:complexity} answers the accuracy half of that question at a budget of $10{,}000$ labels.
$\Phi$satNet reaches $0.3316$ test mIoU against the supervised U-Net's $0.3739$ and the distilled student's $0.4481$, with $28$ times fewer parameters than either.
Per parameter it is far ahead of both; in absolute accuracy it is behind both.
Which of those two readings should govern is a deployment question rather than a scientific one, and under the power and memory budget of an on-board processor it is not obvious that the absolute reading wins.

The more informative result comes from the matched initialisation, which shows that the pre-training contributes almost nothing: fine-tuning from the released land-cover checkpoint beats the identical architecture from a random initialisation by $0.0102$ test mIoU.
The same checkpoint, run on real $\Phi$-sat-2 without any training, scores at the level of a uniform-random predictor.
What $\Phi$satNet contributes in this comparison is its parameter efficiency and not transfer from its mission-specific corpus.

This is not evidence against the claim in~\cite{phisatnet}, which concerns a different benchmark suite.
It is, however, the same problem this thesis is about, displaced one level: the released $\Phi$satNet weights were pre-trained on simulated $\Phi$-sat-2 imagery, and Section~\ref{sec:rq1-results} shows that a gap of this kind originates in low-level radiometry rather than in semantics, which is exactly the part of a sensor a simulator has to get right and the part it is hardest to get right.
A narrow pre-training corpus only earns its narrowness if it matches the sensor that is eventually flown.
Where that corpus is a simulation, the sensor gap becomes a property of the pre-training data itself, and the comparison is less a contest between a general and a specialised model than a restatement of the same measurement problem.
We did not measure the real-to-simulated $\Phi$-sat-2 distance directly, so this reading is an interpretation of the control rather than a result in its own right.

Distillation does not close the gap either, and the reason is instructive.
Handing $\Phi$satNet the same teacher, objective and budget that lift the U-Net by $0.074$ mIoU moves it by nothing at all (Section~\ref{sec:rq3-results}), which places the limit on capacity rather than on supervision.
The choice this poses for an on-board system is therefore not between a well-taught small model and a poorly-taught large one: below some size the teaching stops arriving, and the only way to buy the distilled student's accuracy is to carry its parameters.
Each configuration here is a single seed, so the margins of order $0.01$ separating the $\Phi$satNet variants should be read as the absence of a large effect rather than as measurements of small ones (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Practical recommendations}

These findings suggest three practical recommendations.
First, missions intended to host on-board foundation models should prioritise collecting co-registered calibration data early in their operational life.
The alignement with some existing acquisition from Sentinel-2 made the measurements of this chapter possible.
Second, when the deployed sensor is known at inference, domain-specific batch normalization is a valuable mechanism to bridge the domain gap as it only adds a few thousand additional parameters to the model.
Third, the label budget should be measured before adopting a distillation pipeline, since below the crossover of Section~\ref{sec:label-crossover}, the simpler supervised baseline was more accurate and faster to train.
